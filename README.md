Summary:

Project Description: For this project, we investigated how changes in US interest rates, specifically the Federal Funds Rate, affect stock market behavior, focusing on the S&P 500 index. We used historical data from the past ten years to see whether shifts in interest rates have a strong impact on monthly returns and volatility in the equity market. We also explored how market behavior differs on days when the Federal Reserve shares rate hikes compared to non-hike days. The analysis we completed combines economic data, market data series, feature engineering, statistical summaries, and visualizations to evaluate these relationships. Overall, this work builds on previous milestones in the semester and forms a reproducible workflow presented in this report. 

Motivation: The relationship between US interest rates and stock market performance is an important question in Finance, especially for understanding how financial management affects market behavior. We focus on quantifying how these interest rate changes relate to S&P 500 returns and volatility. By reviewing this connection over the past ten years, a period of time with large economic shifts, we hope to provide information that reflects market behavior well. As students studying Finance, we know understanding these dynamics matters for anyone trying to forecast market reactions, manage risk, or make better investment decisions during periods of adjustment. 

Research Questions: Early in our project, we refined our research scope from a broad 70-year window to a more recent 10-year period to improve relevance to current market conditions. Based on that updated scope, our project focuses on the following questions:

What has been the relationship between monthly S&P 500 returns and the federal interest rates over the last ten years?
How do changes in interest rates impact stock market/S&P 500 volatility?

These questions really guided the structure of our data processing, feature engineering, and visual exploratory analysis. 

Data & Approach: We used two datasets for our project. The first included daily S&P 500 data, which allowed us to compute daily returns, intraday range, and 30-day rolling volatility. The second dataset captured Federal Reserve interest rate information, including the Federal Funds Rate and day-to-day or month-to-month changes. After cleaning and validating both datasets, we merged them on the date field to create a unified dataset. We then resampled this merged data to a monthly frequency to compute monthly returns, monthly volatility, and monthly interest rate changes. Additionally, feature engineering played a big role in our project to create strong comparisons. We created variables such as Log_Return, Volatility_30D, FedFunds_Change, SOFR_Change, and Intraday_Range. We also classified each trading day as either a hike day or non-hike day, which created direct comparisons of market behavior around policy decisions. The analysis of our project mainly focuses on correlation analysis, summary statistics, and different visualizations to quantify the relationships between interest rates and market outcomes. 

High-Level Findings: At a high-level, our findings show that interest rate changes do influence market behavior, but the relationships are weaker than many investors might assume. We observed a weak negative correlation between monthly changes in the Federal Funds Rate and monthly S&P 500 volatility, which was about -0.24. This shows that volatility tends to be lower during months with rate increases, which goes against the typical belief that rate hikes increase uncertainty. An explanation for this is that markers often predict rate hikes in advance, reducing immediate volatility following the formal announcement. Furthermore, returns on rate hike days were more tightly clustered, with fewer extreme values compared to non-hike days. The volatility, which was measured by a 30-day rolling period, was also lower on hike days. Lastly, the correlation between hike size and same-day returns was nearly zero. This implies that larger hikes do not lead to larger market reactions, possibly because of expectations and forward guidance. 

Takeaway: Overall, our analysis suggests that while interest rate changes do influence S&P 500 behavior, the effects are very moderate. Volatility tends to decrease slightly when rates rise, but returns show no strong relationship with rate changes. These findings show the complexity of financial markets, where factors like global events, broader economic conditions, and expectations often outweigh the direct impact of monetary policy. 

Data Profile:

Dataset 1 (Federal Funds Rate): The first dataset of our project focuses on the US Federal Funds Effective Rate, which serves as a benchmark for short-term interest rates. This data was sourced from the Federal Reserve Bank of St.Louis (FRED), which we downloaded in a CSV format. The data covers a broad historical range, but we limited our analysis to data from January 2013 to October 2013 to match our 10-year research window. The dataset was originally reported at a daily frequency, with each observation relating to a specific calendar date. The variables used were “DATE” and “FEDFUNDS”, where the latter represents the daily effective interest rate as a percentage. After loading and filtering the data, we computed a new variable called FedFunds_Change. This captured the month-over-month change in the interest rate. This feature allowed us to see how interest rate changes relate to market behavior in a more consistent time frame aligned with our stock market dataset. 

Dataset 2 (S&P 500 Index): The second dataset contains historical market data for the S&P 500, which we obtained from Investing.com. This dataset includes daily price and volume data covering the same period as the interest rate dataset. It provides a range of variables such as open, high, low, close, adjusted close prices, and daily trading volume. For our analysis, we primarily used the “Adj Close” and “Volume” fields, along with the “Date”. The original frequency of this dataset is also daily, which allowed us to perform both high-resolution and monthly-aggregated analysis. We used this to compute daily returns and volatility measures, which were aggregated into monthly metrics that aligned with our other dataset. This dataset helped us analyze market return and volatility in relation to changes in monetary policy. 

Feature Description: To make comparisons between interest rates and market behavior, we created new variables from our raw data. We started by calculating daily returns for the S&P 500, which we captured in a column called “Change%_Value”. This shows the percent change in the index’s adjusted closing price from one day to the next. To better understand short-term market changes, we also calculated a volatility metric (Volatility_30D), which measures how much daily returns varied over each month. At the monthly level, we added two features. This includes the “SP500_Return”, which tracks how much the index rose or fell each month, and “SP500_Volatility”, which explains how volatile the market was during that month. On the interest rate side, we introduced “FedFunds_Change”, which showed how much the Federal Funds Rate shifted from one month to the next. 

Alignment Strategy: Both datasets started at a daily frequency, but since our research focused on monthly trends, we resampled them to a monthly level. For the S&P 500, we calculated returns using the first and last adjusted closing prices of each month and measured volatility as the standard deviation of daily returns. For interest rates, we averaged the daily values each month and computed the change from the previous month. After resampling, we merged the datasets on the end-of-month date, giving us a clean monthly dataset with rate changes, returns, and volatility. For daily comparisons such as hike and non-hike days, we kept the original daily format and matched only the dates common to both datasets. There were no missing values.

Findings:

Main Findings: The main goal of our analysis was to explore how changes in interest rates relate to S&P 500 returns and volatility. We analyzed whether there are consistent patterns between shifts in Federal Funds Rate and how the stock market behaves on both a monthly and daily basis. The conclusions suggest the relationships are more subtle and dependent on the context of the market, even though many investors assume that rising rates automatically mean weaker market performance and increased volatility. 

Interest Rate Changes and S&P 500 Returns: When looking at the relationship between monthly changes in interest rates and monthly S&P 500 returns, we found no strong or consistent correlation. The correlation coefficient between the two variables was close to 0, implying that returns do not reliably rise or fall in response to rate hikes or cuts. The visualizations confirmed that both positive and negative market returns happened across a wide range of rate change scenarios. This means the direction and size of interest rate moves were not strong predictors of monthly returns. This aligns with the view that financial markets tend to price in expected rate changes ahead of time, and therefore react more to surprises or broader macroeconomic signals. 

Interest Rate Changes and Volatility: The relationship between interest rate changes and market volatility showed a bit more structure. On a monthly level, we saw a weak negative correlation of around -0.24 between rate increases and market volatility. This means that during months when the Federal Funds Rate increased, market volatility tended to be slightly lower on average. This goes against the common idea that rate hikes naturally increase uncertainty. When the Federal Reserve raises rates in a predictable way, it actually signals confidence in the economy, which can minimize short-term anxiety for investors. Also, because markets often predict rate changes in advance, the actual announcement may have less impact on volatility than expected. At the daily level, we also compared S&P 500 behavior on hike days vs. non-hike days. We found that daily returns on hike days were generally more tightly distributed, with fewer extreme outliers, while non-hike days showed greater variability. The 30-day rolling volatility was slightly lower on average hike days as well. On top of this, we looked at the relationship between the rise of rate hikes and same-day returns, finding a correlation close to 0 of about -0.097. This implies that larger hikes do not typically lead to larger market swings, supporting the idea that investor expectations and central bank communication play a larger role than the hike size itself.  

Interpretation: When looking at these together, these findings suggest that the S&P 500 response to interest rate changes does not follow a simple cause-and-effect logic. While interest rates are an important part of macroeconomic policy, the market’s behavior highlights a mix of expectations and other economic factors that aren’t fully captured by rate levels alone. The results of our project show the importance of financial signaling, where how a rate hike is communicated and whether it aligns with market expectations can matter more than the hike itself. 

Caveats/Limitations: It’s important to note some of the limitations of the analysis we did this semester. First, our time horizon was limited to a 10-year period, which excludes many historical rate regimes. This could limit generalizability. Additionally, we focused solely on interest rates, leaving out other macroeconomic variables such as inflation, unemployment, or global market events that could impact market behavior. Finally, while we saw some correlations, our analysis doesn’t imply causation because the relationships we observed are descriptive, not predictive.

Reproducing:

To ensure the reproducibility of our analysis, we have structured our repository with clear documentation, modular code, and automated scripts that can allow anyone to fully replicate our results from start to finish. All components needed to reproduce the workflow are included in the repository or described with instructions. 

The first step would be to clone the repository from GitHub. Inside the main directory, users will find a subfolder for raw and processed data, scripts for data cleaning/analysis, generated outputs, and a figured folder for all visualizations used in the report. All Python libraries are listed in a “requirements.txt” file, and an additional “environment.txt” file is included as a record of the exact package version used via “pip freeze”. To avoid package conflicts, we recommend using a virtual environment before installing packages with “pip install -r requirements.txt”. 

The data sources used in our project are entirely public and accessible. The Federal Funds Effective Rate is gathered from the Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED), and S&P 500 historical data is pulled from Investing.com. We provide a script as “download_data.py”, which automates the download of both datasets and saves them into the “data/raw/” directory. If users prefer or encounter any issues with automated retrieval, we have uploaded a ZIP archive containing both raw and processed data to a public Box folder. A link to this folder is included in our repository, and users are instructed to extract and place the contents in the appropriate data folders. This guarantees reproducibility even if API access is temporarily unavailable. 

After completing data acquisition, users can execute our full analysis pipeline by running a series of scripts provided in the “scr/” directory. The first script, “clean_and_merge.py”, cleans each dataset, resamples daily data to a monthly frequency, engineers relevant features, and outputs a final merged dataset called “merged_dataset.csv” into the “data/processed/” folder. Once the data is prepared, the next script called “generate_visualizations.py” produces the plots and summary figures featured in our report, all of which are stored in the “figures/” directory. Finally, “correlation_analysis.py” performs the statistical comparisons and generates supporting tables such as correlation matrices and grouped return summaries. These are stored in the “output/” folder alongside “run_log.txt” that records timestamps and any error messages. This provides traceability throughout the process. 

The structure of our repository is simple and intuitive. The “data/” directory holds all datasets, split into “raw/” and “processed/”. The “src/” folder contains all Python scripts, which are separated into subfolders for data and analysis tasks. Outputs, such as visualizations and statistical summaries, are saved in “figures/” and “output/” folders. The README.md file contains instructions on folder setup and packages, making it easy for users to follow the full workflow. 

For advanced users, we also provided a “Dockerfile” to containerize the full environment. This allows users to build and run the analysis in a consistent setup regardless of their operating system. We have instructions for this in the README.md file. 

In summary, our project is made to be fully reproducible with minimal effort. From code to data access and execution logs are documented. Users can replicate our analysis by simply downloading the repository, installing packages, and running the included scripts in sequence. Additionally, we offer an optional backup data source via Box to make sure there are no issues with access. Doing this, we reflect the project’s goal of making financial data analysis transparent and accessible.
