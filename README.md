Summary:

Project Description: For this project, we investigated how changes in US interest rates, specifically the Federal Funds Rate, affect stock market behavior, focusing on the S&P 500 index. We used historical data from the past ten years to see whether shifts in interest rates have a strong impact on monthly returns and volatility in the equity market. We also explored how market behavior differs on days when the Federal Reserve shares rate hikes compared to non-hike days. The analysis we completed combines economic data, market data series, feature engineering, statistical summaries, and visualizations to evaluate these relationships. Overall, this work builds on previous milestones in the semester and forms a reproducible workflow presented in this report. 

Motivation: The relationship between US interest rates and stock market performance is an important question in Finance, especially for understanding how financial management affects market behavior. We focus on quantifying how these interest rate changes relate to S&P 500 returns and volatility. By reviewing this connection over the past ten years, a period of time with large economic shifts, we hope to provide information that reflects market behavior well. As students studying Finance, we know understanding these dynamics matters for anyone trying to forecast market reactions, manage risk, or make better investment decisions during periods of adjustment. 

Research Questions: Early in our project, we refined our research scope from a broad 70-year window to a more recent 10-year period to improve relevance to current market conditions. Based on that updated scope, our project focuses on the following questions:

What has been the relationship between monthly S&P 500 returns and the federal interest rates over the last ten years?
How do changes in interest rates impact stock market/S&P 500 volatility?

These questions really guided the structure of our data processing, feature engineering, and visual exploratory analysis. 

Data & Approach: We used two datasets for our project. The first included daily S&P 500 data, which allowed us to compute daily returns, intraday range, and 30-day rolling volatility. The second dataset captured Federal Reserve interest rate information, including the Federal Funds Rate and day-to-day or month-to-month changes. After cleaning and validating both datasets, we merged them on the date field to create a unified dataset. We then resampled this merged data to a monthly frequency to compute monthly returns, monthly volatility, and monthly interest rate changes. Additionally, feature engineering played a big role in our project to create strong comparisons. We created variables such as Log_Return, Volatility_30D, FedFunds_Change, SOFR_Change, and Intraday_Range. We also classified each trading day as either a hike day or non-hike day, which created direct comparisons of market behavior around policy decisions. The analysis of our project mainly focuses on correlation analysis, summary statistics, and different visualizations to quantify the relationships between interest rates and market outcomes. 

High-Level Findings: At a high-level, our findings show that interest rate changes do influence market behavior, but the relationships are weaker than many investors might assume. We observed a weak negative correlation between monthly changes in the Federal Funds Rate and monthly S&P 500 volatility, which was about -0.24. This shows that volatility tends to be lower during months with rate increases, which goes against the typical belief that rate hikes increase uncertainty. An explanation for this is that markers often predict rate hikes in advance, reducing immediate volatility following the formal announcement. Furthermore, returns on rate hike days were more tightly clustered, with fewer extreme values compared to non-hike days. The volatility, which was measured by a 30-day rolling period, was also lower on hike days. Lastly, the correlation between hike size and same-day returns was nearly zero. This implies that larger hikes do not lead to larger market reactions, possibly because of expectations and forward guidance. 

Takeaway: Overall, our analysis suggests that while interest rate changes do influence S&P 500 behavior, the effects are very moderate. Volatility tends to decrease slightly when rates rise, but returns show no strong relationship with rate changes. These findings show the complexity of financial markets, where factors like global events, broader economic conditions, and expectations often outweigh the direct impact of monetary policy. 

Data Profile:

Dataset 1 (Federal Funds Rate): The first dataset of our project focuses on the US Federal Funds Effective Rate, which serves as a benchmark for short-term interest rates. This data was sourced from the Federal Reserve Bank of St.Louis (FRED), which we downloaded in a CSV format. The data covers a broad historical range, but we limited our analysis to data from January 2013 to October 2013 to match our 10-year research window. The dataset was originally reported at a daily frequency, with each observation relating to a specific calendar date. The variables used were “DATE” and “FEDFUNDS”, where the latter represents the daily effective interest rate as a percentage. After loading and filtering the data, we computed a new variable called FedFunds_Change. This captured the month-over-month change in the interest rate. This feature allowed us to see how interest rate changes relate to market behavior in a more consistent time frame aligned with our stock market dataset. 

Dataset 2 (S&P 500 Index): The second dataset contains historical market data for the S&P 500, which we obtained from Yahoo Finance. This dataset includes daily price and volume data covering the same period as the interest rate dataset. It provides a range of variables such as open, high, low, close, adjusted close prices, and daily trading volume. For our analysis, we primarily used the “Adj Close” and “Volume” fields, along with the “Date”. The original frequency of this dataset is also daily, which allowed us to perform both high-resolution and monthly-aggregated analysis. We used this to compute daily returns and volatility measures, which were aggregated into monthly metrics that aligned with our other dataset. This dataset helped us analyze market return and volatility in relation to changes in monetary policy. 

Feature Description: To make comparisons between interest rates and market behavior, we created new variables from our raw data. We started by calculating daily returns for the S&P 500, which we captured in a column called “Change%_Value”. This shows the percent change in the index’s adjusted closing price from one day to the next. To better understand short-term market changes, we also calculated a volatility metric (Volatility_30D), which measures how much daily returns varied over each month. At the monthly level, we added two features. This includes the “SP500_Return”, which tracks how much the index rose or fell each month, and “SP500_Volatility”, which explains how volatile the market was during that month. On the interest rate side, we introduced “FedFunds_Change”, which showed how much the Federal Funds Rate shifted from one month to the next. 

Alignment Strategy: Both datasets started at a daily frequency, but since our research focused on monthly trends, we resampled them to a monthly level. For the S&P 500, we calculated returns using the first and last adjusted closing prices of each month and measured volatility as the standard deviation of daily returns. For interest rates, we averaged the daily values each month and computed the change from the previous month. After resampling, we merged the datasets on the end-of-month date, giving us a clean monthly dataset with rate changes, returns, and volatility. For daily comparisons such as hike and non-hike days, we kept the original daily format and matched only the dates common to both datasets. There were no missing values.
